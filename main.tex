\begin{abstract}
The Acceleration Consortium and Merck KGaA hosted a 2-day virtual hackathon on March 27-28, 2024, bringing together scientists to explore, collaborate, and innovate in the field of Bayesian optimization for the physical sciences. Participants were encouraged to select or develop Bayesian optimization algorithms, apply them to benchmarking tasks, design new benchmarks, create instructional tutorials, and describe real-world applications. With over 100 participants across 69 academic, industry, and government organizations located in 59 cities, 19 countries, and 4 continents, this was a global event. % https://chatgpt.com/share/f6cd733f-1126-4151-86c5-d4b59d158dc3
The outputs from this event, including developed algorithms, benchmarks, and tutorials, will serve as valuable resources for the research community, in addition to the new skills learned and connections formed. Released projects and general information are available at \url{https://ac-bo-hackathon.github.io/} and other locations linked from individual project pages. This event demonstrates the potential of community-driven research efforts to accelerate advances in Bayesian optimization in chemistry and materials science.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\Gls{bo} has emerged as a powerful tool in optimizing complex and expensive-to-evaluate functions, often outperforming traditional search methods in a variety of scientific domains such as optimizing composition and processing parameters to maximize alloy yield strength or identifying synthesis pathways that maximize efficacy of HIV drugs (\cref{fig:intro-bo}). Hackathons help people to connect, gain skills, and flesh out new ideas. In the words of Michelle Duke, the "Hackathon Queen":

\begin{quote}
A hackathon is a short competition where people work together in teams to solve problems and challenges by coming up with solutions and ideas.
\end{quote}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{latex/figures/intro-bo.png}
    \caption{Optimization traces for traditional design of experiment (DoE) methods compared with \gls{bo}, typically outperforms. \Gls{bo} uses a smart model to predict where to look next in an experiment to find the best results with few experiments \cite{baird_building_2023}.}
    \label{fig:intro-bo}
\end{figure}

The goal of the AC BO Hackathon was to leverage the expertise of a diverse, global community to advance the development and application of \gls{bo} techniques for solving critical challenges in the physical sciences. The hackathon also aimed to foster collaboration and knowledge sharing among participants from different backgrounds, including academia, national laboratories, government agencies, and private industry. The event attracted 139 active participants from 39 teams, representing 52 academic institutions, 3 national labs, and 14 companies. Likewise, the participants were located in 59 cities, 19 countries, and 4 continents (\cref{fig:map}). A full list of projects, including links to the corresponding GitHub repositories, submission video, and social media post are provided in \cref{tab:projects}.

\section{Hackathon Details and Setup}

This hackathon was in large part inspired by \citet{jablonka_14_2023} and is one of several materials-focused hackathons in the past \cite{mulholland_hackathon_2015, sparks_insights_2024}. % l.ferguson_conference_2019

Participants were provided with various resources to prepare for the hackathon – this included GitHub classroom assignments with automated feedback, application- and theory-focused videos and tutorials, Python refresher materials, a list of tools to consider using during the hackathon, project submission instructions, and authorship criteria (\cref{fig:preparation}).

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{latex/figures/preparation_blurred.png}
    \caption{A snapshot of \href{https://ac-bo-hackathon.github.io/resources/}{resources listed on the hackathon webpage} such as hackathon orientation, intro to \gls{bo}, and a Python refresher assignment. These resources prepared participants to maximize their time during the two-day synchronous portion of the hackathon and helped level the playing field for participants with varied backgrounds and skill levels. Portions of the screenshot have been blurred to protect potentially identifying information.}
    \label{fig:preparation}
\end{figure*}

One of the unique aspects of this event is that it was hosted in Gather Town, a sort of union between traditional video conferencing software and retro arcade-style avatars and virtual spaces (\cref{fig:gathertown}). Participants create a custom avatar and maneuver in a two-dimensional space. The videos and audio of other participants appear and become audible when nearby, and fade out when far away, simulating an in-person experience. At the beginning of the hackathon, all participants gathered to listen to keynotes in realtime, which were broadcasted via YouTube live and embedded into the Gather Town space. The videos were then \href{https://ac-bo-hackathon.github.io/videos-slides/}{made available on the hackathon website}, which collectively garnered approximately 1600 views within two months. After the keynotes, teams were assigned tables in breakout rooms, each with a whiteboard. Individual tables were assigned as "private spaces" which isolated the shared audio and video within each space. This had a number of advantages for collaboration within and across teams.

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{latex/figures/gathertown.png}
    \caption{Gather town \href{https://ac-bo-hackathon.github.io/videos-slides/}{keynote} room (left), custom avatars (top-right), and an example of a breakout room for teams (bottom-right). Keynotes were broadcasted in realtime to participants via an embedded YouTube livestream. Use of Gather Town helped level the playing field for teams who were in physically separate locations and made it easier for facilitators and other teams to have more natural "check-ins" with other projects.}
    \label{fig:gathertown}
\end{figure*}


The hackathon concluded with a project showcase accompanied by crowdsourced judging within a "poster room" (\cref{fig:poster}).

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{latex/figures/posters.png}
    \caption{The synchronous portion of the hackathon concluded with a poster session and community judging. One participant noted that "it almost felt like a real poster session."}
    \label{fig:poster}
\end{figure*}

Community judging occurred via \href{https://github.com/anishathalye/gavel}{Gavel}, an automated pairwise comparison judging system. Use of this system helped to improve fairness, scalability, and accuracy by having judges compare projects relative to each other rather than assigning subjective numerical scores (for example, "rate your pain on a scale from 1 to 10, where 10 is the worst possible pain you can imagine"). The approach of pairwise judging reduces bias, allows for handling large competitions efficiently, and produces high-quality rankings using statistical models with dynamic assignments to judges to maximize information gain. It has been successfully used at HackMIT and other events to streamline judging and enhance transparency and credibility.

The corresponding Gavel web app was hosted on Heroku according to directions in the Gavel repository. Individualized links were distributed to judges via email using Gavel's SendGrid integration. Collectively, 35 judges cast 319 votes.

\section{Projects' Key Findings}

This section provides a summary of the key findings from all 45 project submissions.
To streamline the process, the audio from each YouTube video submission was transcribed and condensed into a draft summary by an AI agent powered by Anthropic's \emph{claude-3-5-sonnet-20240620} language model with a temperature of 0.3, applied uniformly across submissions so that each project received consistent treatment.
Each draft was then manually reviewed and edited by the organizers to correct errors and ensure a faithful description; the resulting per-project summaries are archived alongside the manuscript source.
We emphasize that these summaries are intended as concise, uniformly generated descriptions of what each team set out to do and reported, rather than as an independent or objective evaluation of the projects' technical merit.
Six projects (14, 19, 23, 29, 34, and 42) did not have a recorded video submission and therefore could not be processed through the transcription pipeline; for these, the summaries below were instead prepared manually from the corresponding project pages and code repositories, as noted at the end of each such summary.

\section{Cross-Project Synthesis}
\label{sec:synthesis}

The per-project summaries above document \emph{what} each team attempted; here we step back to synthesize patterns \emph{across} the 45 submissions and to distill practical and scientific lessons, as encouraged by the reviewers. These observations carry an important caveat: the synchronous hackathon spanned only two days, so most submissions are best understood as rapid feasibility studies rather than validated results. We therefore report qualitative trends and illustrative examples rather than statistically powered conclusions, and we caution against over-interpreting the outcome of any single project.

\subsection{Classification of project outputs}
\label{sec:classification}

To make the collection easier to navigate and reuse, we classified each submission by its \emph{primary} deliverable into one of five categories: mature software, benchmark dataset/problem, tutorial or educational resource, application demonstration, and preliminary concept (\cref{tab:classification}). The distribution is dominated by application demonstrations ($24$ of $45$), with comparatively few released, reusable software tools ($3$)---a pattern consistent with a short event in which most teams probed feasibility rather than completing and validating a finished artifact. The label ``mature software'' denotes a usable, released tool or a contribution to an established library (e.g., input warping and tutorials contributed to \textsc{gauche}~\cite{griffiths_gauche_2024}); it should not be read as evidence of production readiness.

\subsection{Commonalities, differences, and participant expertise}
\label{sec:commonalities}

Across the projects, a common template emerged: frame a chemistry or materials task as sequential optimization of an expensive objective using a Gaussian-process surrogate and an acquisition function, most often built on \textsc{BoTorch}/\textsc{GPyTorch} or Ax, sometimes through domain-oriented wrappers such as \textsc{BayBE}~\cite{fitzner_baybe_2022} or \textsc{gauche}~\cite{griffiths_gauche_2024}. Recurring design choices included expected improvement, upper-confidence-bound, or Thompson-sampling acquisition, and single- versus multi-objective formulations (e.g., expected hypervolume improvement)~\cite{ament_sustainable_2023}. The submissions spanned a balanced mix of domains---molecular and drug discovery, materials and self-driving-laboratory workflows, and generic BO methodology---with smaller clusters on reaction optimization and one on crop genetics.

Teams differed most in three respects: the representation of inputs (physical descriptors versus molecular fingerprints versus learned embeddings), whether they compared against simple baselines such as random search, and whether they targeted real experimental data or retrospective benchmarks. A notable cross-cutting theme was the use of large language models (LLMs) and generative models: under a strict definition, 6 of the 45 projects (and 8 under a broader definition that includes pretrained scientific language-model representations) incorporated such models for design-space specification, preference elicitation, warm-start retrieval, or multi-agent orchestration. This prevalence---roughly one in six projects---mirrors the momentum of dedicated LLM-for-chemistry hackathons~\cite{jablonka_14_2023, zimmermann_reflections_2024} and motivates the outlook in \cref{sec:future}.

Participants ranged from students to senior researchers spanning academia, industry, and government. Consistent with prior scientific hackathons, mixed-expertise teams that paired domain scientists with BO practitioners appeared to be an asset~\cite{valentine_hacktive_2025, busby_building_2015}. We did not conduct a controlled study relating prior expertise to project quality, and because the event was short we caution that apparent performance differences may reflect problem difficulty or the availability of warm-start data rather than expertise or method choice. Likewise, a rigorous longitudinal study of whether participants subsequently adopt BO in their own research is beyond the scope of this report; the broader literature notes that such follow-up data are generally scarce and that many hackathon prototypes do not continue past the event~\cite{nolte_what_2020}. Qualitatively, several outputs have persisted---for example, contributions folded into the maintained \textsc{gauche} library and a community-curated ``awesome'' resource list (project 43)---and a durable community formed around the event, echoing the persistent communities reported for sibling hackathons~\cite{zimmermann_reflections_2024}.

\section{Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:lessons}

\subsection{Organizational lessons}
\label{sec:org-lessons}

Several organizational choices worked well and would be retained. Extensive pre-event preparation---orientation materials, an introduction to BO, curated tooling, and a Python refresher delivered through GitHub Classroom with automated feedback---helped level a wide range of backgrounds (\cref{fig:preparation}). A persistent virtual venue (Gather Town) sustained informal interaction and mentor access in a fully online setting, and a transparent pairwise-comparison judging system (Gavel) scaled expert evaluation across many projects with modest per-judge effort (35 judges cast 319 votes). Archiving every output to GitHub and consolidating them in a single Zenodo record produced a durable, citable community resource. These choices align with best practices identified across scientific hackathons: strong onboarding, structured mentoring, and disciplined post-event archiving~\cite{stoltzfus_community_2017, busby_building_2015, valentine_hacktive_2025}.

Other aspects proved more difficult. Some teams that registered before the event did not converge into active projects, and six teams recorded no closing video, which made their outputs harder to capture---echoing reports that pre-event team formation and mentor engagement are among the most fragile parts of virtual hackathons~\cite{diemar_report_2025}. The compressed two-day window necessarily limited validation depth, and the reproducibility of participant code varied widely (\cref{sec:classification}). In hindsight, we would (i) begin team formation earlier with structured virtual meet-and-greets one to two weeks in advance; (ii) provide explicit reproducibility and licensing checklists (pinned dependencies, a runnable notebook, and an appropriate license) to raise the floor on code quality; (iii) request a short recorded or written summary from every team so that all outputs are captured uniformly; and (iv) communicate evaluation expectations to participants more explicitly. We note that judging used holistic pairwise comparison rather than a numeric scoring rubric; the instructions given to judges together with the submission and authorship requirements that functioned as evaluation criteria are documented on the hackathon website and archived in the Zenodo record.

\subsection{Scientific lessons: strengths and limitations of Bayesian optimization}
\label{sec:sci-lessons}

Taken together, the projects illustrate both the appeal and the practical pitfalls of BO in chemistry and materials, in line with the broader literature. On the strengths side, teams repeatedly exploited BO's sample efficiency in low-data regimes, recovering strong candidates within a handful of iterations relative to design-of-experiments or random baselines~\cite{frazier_tutorial_2018, shahriari_taking_2016}. The submissions collectively demonstrated BO's flexibility, handling continuous, categorical, mixed, molecular, multi-objective, multi-fidelity, contextual, batched, and cost-aware problems, including successful prototypes of multi-objective search and preference-based, human-in-the-loop workflows~\cite{lin_preference_2022}. Specialized kernels and representations (e.g., for molecules) further extended BO to structured inputs~\cite{griffiths_gauche_2024}.

The projects also surfaced well-known limitations. Default BO was not always superior to simple baselines: in project 33, standard GP-based BO was comparable to random search, and in the narrow benchmark of project 1 an off-the-shelf method struggled against random candidates---underscoring the importance of baseline comparisons and careful benchmarking~\cite{liang_benchmarking_2021}. Results were sensitive to molecular representation, featurization, and kernel choice; warm-start data quality, observation noise, and high-dimensional search spaces were recurring pain points, for which noise-robust and heteroscedastic-aware methods are relevant remedies~\cite{aldeghi_golem_2021, altamirano_robust_2023}. Acquisition-function optimization itself mattered: one of the top-ranked projects (project 3) showed that investing more computation in the acquisition optimizer changed observed performance, connecting to ongoing work on scalable and high-dimensional acquisition strategies~\cite{ament_sustainable_2023, maus_local_2023}. Finally, retrospective benchmark gains do not guarantee wet-laboratory success, and reproducing comparisons across studies remains difficult~\cite{liang_benchmarking_2021}. Where teams integrated LLMs, the models lowered access barriers and helped specify spaces or retrieve priors, but they added another uncertain component that can degrade on complex objectives and requires validation against non-LLM baselines~\cite{kristiadi_sober_2024}.

On the recurring question of which framework works best for a given noise regime, the projects and literature suggest there is no universal winner. For low-noise, near-deterministic objectives, standard GP-based BO with expected improvement remains a reasonable default, whereas noisy, heteroscedastic, or multi-objective settings benefit from noise-robust acquisitions and tooling, and deployment-oriented libraries such as \textsc{BayBE} and \textsc{BoFire} target the experimental setting specifically~\cite{fitzner_baybe_2022, durholt_bofire_2024}. We again emphasize the two-day caveat: these are directional observations drawn from short feasibility studies, not benchmark verdicts.

\section{Future Opportunities}
\label{sec:future}

Building on the hackathon outputs and the trends above, we highlight several promising directions for BO in chemistry and materials discovery. \emph{Autonomous and self-driving laboratories} are a natural home for BO, closing the loop between algorithmic suggestion and automated experimentation~\cite{tom_self-driving_2024, abolhasani_rise_2023}. \emph{Multi-fidelity optimization} that fuses cheap simulations or proxy measurements with expensive experiments can substantially reduce cost, provided its assumptions are handled carefully~\cite{sabanza-gil_best_2025, judge_applying_2024}. \emph{Uncertainty-aware experimental planning} and active learning under design constraints can make campaigns more sample-efficient and trustworthy~\cite{low_evolution_2024}.

The large share of hackathon projects that already incorporated language models points to \emph{foundation-model- and LLM-assisted BO} as a particularly active frontier---for design-space specification, prior construction, and warm-starting---though recent work cautions that LLM-based surrogates and priors do not yet consistently outperform strong classical baselines and must be benchmarked rigorously~\cite{kristiadi_sober_2024, rankovic_gollum_2025}. Other opportunities include \emph{preference-based and human-in-the-loop BO} for subjective or hard-to-quantify objectives~\cite{adachi_looping_2024, deneault_preferential_2025}; \emph{multi-objective materials design} with scalable hypervolume-based acquisitions~\cite{ament_sustainable_2023}; \emph{robust BO under noisy experimental data}~\cite{aldeghi_golem_2021, altamirano_robust_2023}; \emph{community benchmark development} to enable reproducible, apples-to-apples comparisons~\cite{liang_benchmarking_2021, felton_summit_2021}; and \emph{domain-specific, deployment-ready BO tools} for synthesis and processing optimization that meet experimentalists where they work~\cite{durholt_bofire_2024, fitzner_baybe_2022}. Collectively, these directions echo the broader perspective that Bayesian methods are becoming a core component of the autonomous materials-discovery toolkit~\cite{arroyave_perspective_2022}, while the hackathon itself illustrates both the promise and the present-day rough edges that motivate continued community effort.
