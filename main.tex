\begin{abstract}
The Acceleration Consortium and Merck KGaA hosted a 2-day virtual hackathon on March 27-28, 2024, bringing together scientists to explore, collaborate, and innovate in the field of Bayesian optimization for the physical sciences. Participants were encouraged to select or develop Bayesian optimization algorithms, apply them to benchmarking tasks, design new benchmarks, create instructional tutorials, and describe real-world applications. With over 100 participants across 69 academic, industry, and government organizations located in 59 cities, 19 countries, and 4 continents, this was a global event. % https://chatgpt.com/share/f6cd733f-1126-4151-86c5-d4b59d158dc3
The outputs from this event, including developed algorithms, benchmarks, and tutorials, will serve as valuable resources for the research community, in addition to the new skills learned and connections formed. Released projects and general information are available at \url{https://ac-bo-hackathon.github.io/} and other locations linked from individual project pages. This event demonstrates the potential of community-driven research efforts to accelerate advances in Bayesian optimization in chemistry and materials science.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\Gls{bo} has emerged as a powerful tool in optimizing complex and expensive-to-evaluate functions, often outperforming traditional search methods in a variety of scientific domains such as optimizing composition and processing parameters to maximize alloy yield strength or identifying synthesis pathways that maximize efficacy of HIV drugs (\cref{fig:intro-bo}). Hackathons help people to connect, gain skills, and flesh out new ideas. In the words of Michelle Duke, the "Hackathon Queen":

\begin{quote}
A hackathon is a short competition where people work together in teams to solve problems and challenges by coming up with solutions and ideas.
\end{quote}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{latex/figures/intro-bo.png}
    \caption{Optimization traces for traditional design of experiment (DoE) methods compared with \gls{bo}, typically outperforms. \Gls{bo} uses a smart model to predict where to look next in an experiment to find the best results with few experiments \cite{baird_building_2023}.}
    \label{fig:intro-bo}
\end{figure}

The goal of the AC BO Hackathon was to leverage the expertise of a diverse, global community to advance the development and application of \gls{bo} techniques for solving critical challenges in the physical sciences. The hackathon also aimed to foster collaboration and knowledge sharing among participants from different backgrounds, including academia, national laboratories, government agencies, and private industry. The event attracted 139 active participants from 39 teams, representing 52 academic institutions, 3 national labs, and 14 companies. Likewise, the participants were located in 59 cities, 19 countries, and 4 continents (\cref{fig:map}). A full list of projects, including links to the corresponding GitHub repositories, submission video, and social media post are provided in \cref{tab:projects}.

\section{Hackathon Details and Setup}

This hackathon was in large part inspired by \citet{jablonka_14_2023} and is one of several materials-focused hackathons in the past \cite{mulholland_hackathon_2015, sparks_insights_2024}. % l.ferguson_conference_2019

Participants were provided with various resources to prepare for the hackathon: GitHub classroom assignments with automated feedback, application- and theory-focused videos and tutorials, Python refresher materials, a list of tools to consider using during the hackathon, project submission instructions, and authorship criteria (\cref{fig:preparation}).

One of the unique aspects of this event is that it was hosted in Gather Town, a sort of union between traditional video conferencing software and retro arcade-style avatars and virtual spaces (\cref{fig:gathertown}). Participants create a custom avatar and maneuver in a two-dimensional space. The videos and audio of other participants appear and become audible when nearby, and fade out when far away, simulating an in-person experience. At the beginning of the hackathon, all participants gathered to listen to keynotes in realtime, which were broadcasted via YouTube live and embedded into the Gather Town space. The videos were then \href{https://ac-bo-hackathon.github.io/videos-slides/}{made available on the hackathon website}, which collectively garnered approximately 1600 views within two months. After the keynotes, teams were assigned tables in breakout rooms, each with a whiteboard. Individual tables were assigned as "private spaces" which isolated the shared audio and video within each space. This had a number of advantages for collaboration within and across teams.

The hackathon concluded with a project showcase accompanied by crowdsourced judging within a "poster room" (\cref{fig:poster}).

Community judging occurred via \href{https://github.com/anishathalye/gavel}{Gavel}, an automated pairwise comparison judging system. Use of this system helped to improve fairness, scalability, and accuracy by having judges compare projects relative to each other rather than assigning subjective numerical scores (for example, "rate your pain on a scale from 1 to 10, where 10 is the worst possible pain you can imagine"). Rather than scoring against a numeric rubric, judges answered a single holistic question for each assigned pair, effectively "which of these two projects is better?". The approach of pairwise judging reduces bias, allows for handling large competitions efficiently, and produces high-quality rankings using statistical models with dynamic assignments to judges to maximize information gain. It has been successfully used at HackMIT and other events to streamline judging and enhance transparency and credibility. See \cref{tab:winners} for rankings of the top 10 projects and the awarded prizes.

The corresponding Gavel web app was hosted on Heroku according to directions in the Gavel repository. Individualized links were distributed to judges via email using Gavel's SendGrid integration. Collectively, 35 judges cast 319 votes.

\section{Projects' Key Findings}

This section provides a summary of the key findings from all 45 project submissions.
To streamline the process, the speech transcript of each YouTube video submission was retrieved and condensed into a draft summary by an AI agent powered by Anthropic's \emph{claude-3-5-sonnet-20240620} language model with a temperature of 0.3, applied uniformly across submissions.
Each draft was then reviewed and edited by the organizers to correct errors and ensure a faithful description; the per-project summaries are archived alongside the manuscript source.
Six projects (14, 19, 23, 29, 34, and 42) had no recorded video submission to transcribe; their summaries were instead prepared from the corresponding project pages and each team's public artifacts, as noted at the end of each such summary.

\section{Cross-Project Synthesis}
\label{sec:synthesis}

The per-project summaries above document \emph{what} each team attempted; here we step back to synthesize patterns across the 45 submissions. Given that the synchronous hackathon spanned two days, many of these projects did not undergo the same rigor and fleshing out as what might be expected of a peer-reviewed manuscript. However, we report qualitative trends and examples.

We classified each submission by its \emph{primary} deliverable into one of five categories: mature software, benchmark dataset/problem, tutorial or educational resource, application demonstration, and preliminary concept (\cref{tab:classification}). The distribution is dominated by application demonstrations ($22$ of $45$), with comparatively few released, reusable software tools ($3$). The label ``mature software'' denotes a usable, released tool or a contribution to an established library such as \textsc{gauche}~\cite{griffiths_gauche_2024}.

This deliverable-based grouping cuts across the five special topics under which projects were submitted (\cref{tab:project_topics}): of the 45 project pages, 20 record the open-ended general topic, 9 propose real-world tasks, 5 apply algorithms to benchmark tasks, 4 create tutorials, 2 develop benchmarks, and 5 record no topic. How many submissions landed in the general category surprised the organizers, who would not have known in advance how best to segment the topics; a special topic such as ``large language models applied to Bayesian optimization'' would likely have been among the largest had it been offered, given the LLM prevalence noted below.

\end{table}

Methodologically, a common template emerged: frame a chemistry or materials task as sequential optimization of an expensive objective with a Gaussian-process surrogate and an acquisition function, most often built on \textsc{BoTorch}/Ax, sometimes through domain wrappers such as \textsc{BayBE}~\cite{fitzner_baybe_2022} or \textsc{gauche}~\cite{griffiths_gauche_2024}. Teams differed most in input representation, in whether they compared against simple baselines such as random search, and in whether they targeted real experimental data or retrospective benchmarks. Participants ranged from students to senior researchers across academia, industry, and government, and mixed-expertise teams pairing domain scientists with BO practitioners appeared to be an asset~\cite{valentine_hacktive_2025, busby_building_2015}. Roughly one in six projects (6 of 45 under a strict definition, 8 under a broader one) incorporated large language models, mirroring dedicated LLM-for-chemistry hackathons~\cite{jablonka_14_2023, zimmermann_reflections_2024} and motivating the outlook in \cref{sec:future}.

\section{Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:lessons}

\subsection{Organizational lessons}
\label{sec:org-lessons}

Several organizational choices worked well and would be retained. Extensive pre-event preparation (orientation materials, an introduction to BO, curated tooling, and a Python refresher delivered through GitHub Classroom with automated feedback) helped level a wide range of backgrounds (\cref{fig:preparation}). A persistent virtual venue (Gather Town) sustained informal interaction and mentor access in a fully online setting, and a transparent pairwise-comparison judging system (Gavel) scaled expert evaluation across many projects with modest per-judge effort (35 judges cast 319 votes). Archiving every output to GitHub and consolidating them in a single Zenodo record produced a durable, citable community resource, and several outputs have persisted past the event, including contributions folded into the maintained \textsc{gauche} library and a community-curated resource list (project 43)~\cite{zimmermann_reflections_2024}. These choices align with best practices identified across scientific hackathons: strong onboarding, structured mentoring, and disciplined post-event archiving~\cite{stoltzfus_community_2017, busby_building_2015, valentine_hacktive_2025}.

Other aspects proved more difficult. Some teams that registered before the event did not converge into active projects, and six teams recorded no closing video, which made their outputs harder to capture; pre-event team formation and mentor engagement are reportedly among the most fragile parts of virtual hackathons~\cite{diemar_report_2025}. The missing videos did not track project quality: the first- and second-place projects were among the six without one. The compressed two-day window limited validation depth, and the reproducibility of participant code varied widely (\cref{sec:synthesis}). In hindsight, we would (i) begin team formation earlier with structured virtual meet-and-greets one to two weeks in advance; (ii) provide explicit reproducibility and licensing checklists (pinned dependencies, a runnable notebook, and an appropriate license) to raise the floor on code quality; (iii) request a short recorded or written summary from every team so that all outputs are captured uniformly; (iv) communicate evaluation expectations to participants more explicitly; and (v) run a follow-up survey of participants, since without one we cannot report whether the event changed how participants use BO in their own research. Judging used holistic pairwise comparison rather than a numeric rubric (see Hackathon Details and Setup for the judging question); the submission and authorship requirements that framed evaluation are documented on the hackathon website and archived in the Zenodo record.

\subsection{Scientific lessons: strengths and limitations of Bayesian optimization}
\label{sec:sci-lessons}

Taken together, the projects illustrate both the appeal and the practical pitfalls of BO in chemistry and materials, in line with the broader literature. On the strengths side, teams repeatedly exploited BO's sample efficiency in low-data regimes, recovering strong candidates within a handful of iterations relative to design-of-experiments or random baselines~\cite{frazier_tutorial_2018, shahriari_taking_2016}. The submissions collectively demonstrated BO's flexibility, handling continuous, categorical, mixed, molecular, multi-objective, multi-fidelity, contextual, batched, and cost-aware problems, including successful prototypes of multi-objective search and preference-based, human-in-the-loop workflows~\cite{lin_preference_2022}. Specialized kernels and representations (e.g., for molecules) further extended BO to structured inputs~\cite{griffiths_gauche_2024}.

The projects also surfaced well-known limitations. Default BO was not always superior to simple baselines: two teams reported little advantage over random search on their tasks (projects 33 and 1), and while these two-day comparisons were not run at benchmark rigor, they underscore the value of always including a simple baseline~\cite{liang_benchmarking_2021}. Results were sensitive to molecular representation, featurization, and kernel choice; warm-start data quality, observation noise, and high-dimensional search spaces were recurring pain points, for which noise-robust and heteroscedastic-aware methods are relevant remedies~\cite{aldeghi_golem_2021, altamirano_robust_2023}. Acquisition-function optimization itself mattered: one of the top-ranked projects (project 3) showed that investing more computation in the acquisition optimizer changed observed performance, connecting to ongoing work on scalable and high-dimensional acquisition strategies~\cite{ament_sustainable_2023, maus_local_2023}. Finally, retrospective benchmark gains do not guarantee wet-laboratory success, and reproducing comparisons across studies remains difficult~\cite{liang_benchmarking_2021}. Where teams integrated LLMs, the models lowered access barriers and helped specify spaces or retrieve priors, but they added another uncertain component that can degrade on complex objectives and requires validation against non-LLM baselines~\cite{kristiadi_sober_2024}.

On the recurring question of which framework works best for a given noise regime, these projects support only a narrow observation: in project 23, modeling the observation noise in the surrogate became more valuable as the noise grew. More broadly, deployment-oriented libraries such as \textsc{BayBE} and \textsc{BoFire} target the experimental setting specifically~\cite{fitzner_baybe_2022, durholt_bofire_2024}; a general ranking of frameworks by noise regime is beyond what a two-day event can establish.

\section{Future Opportunities}
\label{sec:future}

Building on the hackathon outputs and the trends above, we highlight several promising directions for BO in chemistry and materials discovery. \emph{Autonomous and self-driving laboratories} are a natural home for BO, closing the loop between algorithmic suggestion and automated experimentation~\cite{tom_self-driving_2024, abolhasani_rise_2023}. \emph{Multi-fidelity optimization} that fuses cheap simulations or proxy measurements with expensive experiments can substantially reduce cost, provided its assumptions are handled carefully~\cite{sabanza-gil_best_2025, judge_applying_2024}. \emph{Uncertainty-aware experimental planning} and active learning under design constraints can make campaigns more sample-efficient and trustworthy~\cite{low_evolution_2024}.

The large share of hackathon projects that already incorporated language models points to \emph{foundation-model- and LLM-assisted BO} as a particularly active frontier for design-space specification, prior construction, and warm-starting; reported evidence on whether LLM-based surrogates and priors outperform strong classical baselines is mixed, and benchmarking such hybrids fairly (separating genuine generalization from memorized results) is itself an open problem~\cite{kristiadi_sober_2024, rankovic_gollum_2025}. Other opportunities include \emph{preference-based and human-in-the-loop BO} for subjective or hard-to-quantify objectives~\cite{adachi_looping_2024, deneault_preferential_2025}; \emph{multi-objective materials design} with scalable hypervolume-based acquisitions~\cite{ament_sustainable_2023}; \emph{robust BO under noisy experimental data}~\cite{aldeghi_golem_2021, altamirano_robust_2023}; \emph{community benchmark development} to enable reproducible, apples-to-apples comparisons~\cite{liang_benchmarking_2021, felton_summit_2021}; and \emph{domain-specific, deployment-ready BO tools} for synthesis and processing optimization that meet experimentalists where they work, including a growing set of graphical and web-based BO interfaces that remove coding barriers~\cite{durholt_bofire_2024, fitzner_baybe_2022, ishii_boxvia_2022, torres_multi-objective_2022, tamura_nims-os_2023}. Collectively, these directions echo the broader perspective that Bayesian methods are becoming a core component of the autonomous materials-discovery toolkit~\cite{arroyave_perspective_2022}.
