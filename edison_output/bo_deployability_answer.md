# Are Bayesian Optimization Tools Easily Deployable by Experimentalists in Real Laboratory Settings? A Literature Review (2019–2026)

## 1. Introduction

Bayesian optimization (BO) has emerged as a powerful strategy for guiding experimental design in chemistry, materials science, and bioprocess engineering, offering principled exploration of high-dimensional parameter spaces with minimal experimental trials (shields2021bayesianreactionoptimization pages 1-2, liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 1-3). Despite growing algorithmic sophistication, a persistent question is whether BO tools are practically deployable by bench scientists who lack specialized programming or machine-learning training. This review synthesizes evidence from approximately 20 primary sources (2019–2026) across four themes: (1) documented wet-lab deployments, (2) reported barriers to adoption, (3) accessible interfaces and educational resources that lower those barriers, and (4) remaining gaps.

The landmark study by Shields et al. (2021) introduced EDBO (Experimental Design via Bayesian Optimization), a general-purpose open-source tool employing Gaussian process surrogates with a Matérn-5/2 kernel and expected improvement acquisition, and demonstrated its superiority over expert-guided optimization for palladium-catalyzed direct arylation, Mitsunobu, and deoxyfluorination reactions (shen2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 10-11, shields2021bayesianreactionoptimization pages 1-2). Torres et al. (2022) extended this work with EDBO+, incorporating multi-objective BO (q-Expected Hypervolume Improvement) for simultaneous yield and enantioselectivity optimization of Ni/photoredox-catalyzed cross-electrophile coupling; critically, they packaged the optimizer as a no-install web application to remove programming barriers (torres2022amultiobjectiveactive pages 1-2, shen2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 10-11).

In materials science, Liang et al. (2021) benchmarked BO across five experimental domains—including carbon nanotube polymer composites, silver nanoparticles, lead-halide perovskites, and additively manufactured structures—showing that GP with anisotropic (ARD) kernels and random forests both substantially outperform random search (liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 1-3, liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 3-6). Gongora et al. (2020) demonstrated a nearly 60-fold reduction in experiments needed for mechanical design optimization using their Bayesian Experimental Autonomous Researcher (BEAR) (liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 18-21). In thin-film growth, Wakabayashi et al. (2022) achieved record-performance SrRuO₃ films in only 35 MBE runs using a custom BO algorithm that handles experimental failures (liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 18-21).

In drug discovery, McDonald et al. (2025) integrated multi-fidelity BO into an autonomous platform, docking over 3,500 molecules, automatically synthesizing and screening more than 120, and discovering novel submicromolar HDAC inhibitors (shen2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 14-16). Multiple self-driving laboratory (SDL) demonstrations have also employed BO, including continuous-flow reaction optimization by Schilter et al. (2024) achieving >80% conversion for four substrates in only 23 experiments (shen2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 16-17), and the BRINE electrochemical SDL by Ramezani et al. (2026), which tested ~230 electrolyte compositions in under 18 hours (shen2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 16-17). A comprehensive review by Shen et al. (2025) catalogued dozens of additional BO-driven wet-lab campaigns spanning catalyst screening, C–H activation, carbohydrate protecting-group chemistry, and continuous-flow synthesis (shen2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 16-17, shen2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 11-14).

Software tools most frequently cited in these campaigns include EDBO/EDBO+, Dragonfly, PHOENICS, Gryffin, BoTorch/Ax, TSEMO, and ChemOS (shen2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 16-17, shen2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 6-8, deshwal2021bayesianoptimizationof pages 30-32).

## 3. Reported Barriers to Adoption

Despite growing success stories, multiple barriers hinder routine BO adoption by experimentalists.

**Programming expertise.** The most consistently cited obstacle is the requirement for intermediate to advanced Python programming and machine-learning knowledge. Baird et al. (2026) note that existing BO libraries such as Ax and BoTorch impose a steep learning curve, with library-specific conventions and code structures that are unclear to new users and tutorials oriented toward benchmarking rather than experimentalist workflows (baird2502honegumianinterface pages 1-3). Cao et al. (2026) observe that frameworks like BoTorch, Ax, and GPyOpt "require substantial machine learning expertise" and provide limited guidance on algorithm selection and hyperparameter tuning (cao2026bgolearnaunified pages 1-4). Bertelsen et al. (2025) confirm that "applying Bayesian optimization traditionally requires high levels of expertise in coding and machine learning, which limits accessibility for many scientists" (bertelsen2025processoptimizeranopensource pages 1-2). Siska et al. (2026) emphasize that available BO packages still require intermediate programming skills and documentation designed for ML specialists rather than domain practitioners such as bioprocess engineers (siska2026aguideto pages 14-16).

**Problem formulation and search-space specification.** Defining surrogate models, acquisition functions, stopping criteria, and encoding experimental variables as machine-readable inputs are non-trivial for non-experts (siska2026aguideto pages 14-16, siska2026aguideto pages 1-2). Paulson and Tsay (2025) highlight that real-world experiments typically involve hybrid discrete/continuous search spaces, multi-objective trade-offs, black-box constraints, and batch experimental settings—each requiring specialized GP kernels or acquisition function modifications that standard BO methods do not address out of the box (paulson2025bayesianoptimizationas pages 4-7). Shields et al. (2021) similarly noted that at the time of their work, BO had not been systematically applied to typical batch chemistry problems involving mixed discrete and continuous parameters (shields2021bayesianreactionoptimization pages 2-3).

**Software installation and data infrastructure.** Torres et al. (2022) developed a web application specifically because prior tools required local software installation, creating a programming barrier for everyday lab use (torres2022amultiobjectiveactive pages 1-2). Mroz et al. (2025) emphasize that most BO implementations require "bespoke scripts for each optimisation task" and lack seamless integration with electronic lab notebooks (ELNs) via graphical interfaces (mroz2025webbotowardsincreased pages 1-2, mroz2025webbotowardsincreased pages 2-5).

**Instrument integration.** Seifrid et al. (2022) identify a critical challenge: most instrument manufacturers do not design their software with self-driving laboratories in mind, resulting in a lack of comprehensive APIs for external control, which forces researchers to invest significant time writing custom code and introduces potential failure points (seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 8-9, seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 1-2). Roch et al. (2020) note that the manual implementation of communication protocols for each piece of hardware cannot be automated because APIs vary across platforms (roch2020chemosanorchestration pages 3-6, roch2020chemosanorchestration pages 2-3). Salazar-Villacis and Benyahia (2026) add that many laboratory instruments lack mature information models, and legacy equipment creates maintenance complications through version drift, though emerging standards such as OPC UA and SiLA 2 are beginning to address interoperability (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 3-4, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 1-2).

**Educational gaps.** Siska et al. (2026) note that bioprocess engineers typically lack training in BO methods, associated mathematics, and programming, and recommend targeted training programs and interdisciplinary degree programs combining domain science with computer science (siska2026aguideto pages 14-16, siska2026aguideto pages 1-2). Guo et al. (2023) observe that chemistry remains primarily a "wet-lab science" with historical reliance on physical notebooks, creating challenges in data parsing and integration with computational workflows (guo2023bayesianoptimizationfor pages 6-7).

**Code-template generators.** Honegumi (Baird et al., 2026) provides an interactive web-based selection grid that allows users to configure key BO parameters—including objective type, variable types, and constraints—and generates ready-to-use, unit-tested Python scripts built on the Ax platform. Accompanying tutorials bridge conceptual and practical understanding, enabling researchers to translate their problem statements into proper BO configurations without deep knowledge of the underlying library codebase (baird2502honegumianinterface pages 5-7, baird2502honegumianinterface pages 1-3).

**Web applications.** Web-BO (Mroz et al., 2025) offers a browser-based GUI supporting BO for chemical optimization tasks, with integration to the datalab ELN platform. Users upload datasets in CSV or ELN format, define optimization experiments through interactive forms, and receive next-experiment recommendations—all without writing code. The modular architecture supports multiple ELN backends and stores optimization experiments in a searchable SQL database (mroz2025webbotowardsincreased pages 5-7, mroz2025webbotowardsincreased pages 7-9, mroz2025webbotowardsincreased pages 2-5). The EDBO+ web application (Torres et al., 2022) similarly requires no software installation and includes features for on-the-fly condition modification and data visualization, enabling chemists to integrate BO into everyday laboratory practices (torres2022amultiobjectiveactive pages 1-2).

**GUIs and simplified APIs.** Bgolearn's BgoFace GUI (Cao et al., 2026) removes programming barriers entirely while automatically generating equivalent Python code for reproducibility. Common BO workflows are reduced to 3–5 lines of code through a unified, materials-oriented API (cao2026bgolearnaunified pages 1-4, cao2026bgolearnaunified pages 4-6). NEXTorch (Wang et al., 2021) builds on BoTorch but provides automatic parameter scaling, data-type conversions, visualization capabilities, and human-in-the-loop optimization modes specifically designed for chemical sciences and engineering applications (wang2021nextorchadesign pages 2-3, wang2021nextorchadesign pages 1-2, wang2021nextorchadesign pages 6-7). ProcessOptimizer (Bertelsen et al., 2025) uses an intuitive ask()/tell() interface with built-in plotting, benchmarking, and noise-handling capabilities, deliberately prioritizing ease of use for experimentalists with minimal Python training over cutting-edge mathematical sophistication (bertelsen2025processoptimizeranopensource pages 1-2, bertelsen2025processoptimizeranopensource pages 2-3, bertelsen2025processoptimizeranopensource pages 4-5).

**Benchmarks.** Olympus (Häse et al., 2021) provides a standardized framework for benchmarking 18 different experiment planning strategies against 10 experimentally derived datasets and 23 analytical surfaces, enabling researchers to evaluate optimization algorithms on realistic noisy experimental tasks before deploying them in the laboratory (hase2021olympusabenchmarking pages 2-3, hase2021olympusabenchmarking pages 3-4, hase2021olympusabenchmarking pages 1-2). Liang et al. (2021) benchmarked surrogate model and acquisition function pairs across five experimental materials science domains, offering practical guidelines: GP with ARD kernels demonstrated the most robustness, while random forests warrant more consideration as a distribution-free alternative with smaller time complexity (liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 1-3, liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 3-6, liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 18-21). Most recently, Stuckner et al. (2026) compared five BO framework/surrogate combinations (Ax GP, BayBE GP, BayBE neural network, Scipy+Lolopy RF, and Hyperopt TPE) across discrete, continuous, and hybrid materials design benchmarks, recommending BayBE's GP with expected improvement as the reliable default (stuckner2026benchmarkingbayesianoptimization pages 1-6, stuckner2026benchmarkingbayesianoptimization pages 6-8, stuckner2026benchmarkingbayesianoptimization pages 24-26).

## 5. Remaining Gaps Between BO Software Maturity and Routine Laboratory Deployment

Despite substantial progress, several gaps persist.

**Scalability and problem complexity.** Standard BO methods remain limited to roughly 10 dimensions and struggle with the simultaneous occurrence of multi-objective optimization, black-box constraints, multi-fidelity data, and batch experimental designs that characterize real-world laboratory problems (paulson2025bayesianoptimizationas pages 7-10, paulson2025bayesianoptimizationas pages 4-7). Current batch experimental design methods are restricted to small batches (≤10 samples), limiting applicability to high-throughput experimental setups, and asynchronous batch settings with varying observation delays remain an open research direction (paulson2025bayesianoptimizationas pages 7-10). Paulson and Tsay (2025) note that most existing BO methods are suboptimal compared to dynamic programming solutions and that suboptimality bounds are needed to guide practitioners in selecting appropriate methods (paulson2025bayesianoptimizationas pages 10-14).

**Instrument APIs and standardization.** The lack of comprehensive, standardized APIs from instrument manufacturers remains a fundamental bottleneck (seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 8-9, seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 1-2). Seifrid et al. (2022) emphasize that attempting to "hack" instruments without sufficient APIs introduces potential failure points, and few analytical instruments (e.g., mass spectrometry, NMR) have automated analysis conditions that can be established generally (seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 8-9). Salazar-Villacis and Benyahia (2026) argue that interfacing platforms across the development chain requires collaborative efforts among equipment manufacturers, software developers, and regulatory bodies, with investment in interoperability standards such as OPC UA and SiLA 2 (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 12-13, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 1-2). The field also needs common data formats, shared ontologies, and provenance frameworks (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 12-13).

**Reproducibility and data management.** Ramos et al. (2026) identify that LLM-based surrogates for BO have calibration issues limiting uncertainty estimation accuracy, hallucination risks producing infeasible experimental parameters, and inherent stochasticity preventing reproducibility—particularly with closed-source models (ramos2026bayesianoptimizationof pages 9-10). More broadly, FAIR-compliant data provenance, quantitative error-rate reporting, and transparent failure-mode documentation are needed for credibility of autonomous experiments (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 9-11).

**Cultural and educational transformation.** Guo et al. (2023) note that the transition from traditional wet-lab practice to data-driven workflows requires cultural and infrastructural changes in how experimental data is recorded and managed (guo2023bayesianoptimizationfor pages 6-7). Siska et al. (2026) recommend that broader adoption will require not only user-friendly tools but also targeted training programs, domain-relevant tutorials and examples, and interdisciplinary degree programs combining domain science with computational skills (siska2026aguideto pages 14-16, siska2026aguideto pages 16-17). Currently, even the most user-friendly BO packages still require some Python proficiency, and no tool yet provides a fully no-code, plug-and-play experience equivalent to the graphical commercial software available for traditional design of experiments (siska2026aguideto pages 14-16).

**Safety and regulatory considerations.** For sensitive domains such as catalysis and pharmaceutical manufacturing, design-space construction requires careful expert oversight to avoid safety hazards, and existing regulatory frameworks (GLP, GMP) may need revision to account for robotic operation and autonomous decision-making (ramos2026bayesianoptimizationof pages 9-10, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 9-11).

## 6. Conclusions

The evidence indicates that BO tools have progressed considerably from purely computational demonstrations to documented real-laboratory deployments across organic synthesis, materials discovery, catalysis, drug discovery, and bioprocess engineering. Open-source packages (EDBO, Dragonfly, PHOENICS, Gryffin, BoTorch) and orchestration platforms (ChemOS) have enabled these deployments. A new wave of accessible interfaces—including web apps (Web-BO, EDBO+ web app), GUIs (BgoFace), code-template generators (Honegumi), simplified APIs (ProcessOptimizer, NEXTorch), and benchmarking frameworks (Olympus)—is actively lowering the barriers of programming expertise, software installation, and algorithm selection. Nevertheless, routine deployment remains limited by the need for at least basic Python proficiency, the absence of standardized instrument APIs and data infrastructure, scalability challenges in high-dimensional and multi-objective settings, and the cultural transition from notebook-based to data-driven experimental workflows. Closing these gaps will require coordinated effort among BO algorithm developers, instrument manufacturers, standards bodies, and the scientific education community.
