# The Interplay between Large Language Models and Bayesian Optimization for Materials Discovery, Chemistry, and Experimental Design: A Literature Review (2024–2026)

## Overview

The convergence of large language models (LLMs) and Bayesian optimization (BO) represents one of the most active frontiers in computational chemistry and materials science. LLMs bring broad domain knowledge, natural-language reasoning, and zero-shot generalization, while BO provides principled uncertainty quantification and sample-efficient sequential decision-making. The literature from 2024–2026 reveals a rich ecosystem of approaches that ranges from using LLMs as standalone optimizers, through hybrid frameworks that blend LLM priors with GP-based BO, to fully agentic closed-loop laboratory systems. ## 1. LLMs as Direct Optimizers or Experiment Proposers versus Classical BO

The foundational idea of using LLMs as black-box optimizers originates from Yang et al.'s Optimization by Prompting (OPRO), which demonstrated that LLMs can iteratively generate improved solutions when given a natural-language description of the optimization problem and a history of prior evaluations (macknight2025pretrainedknowledgeelevates pages 1-3). Although OPRO was not chemistry-specific, it inspired a wave of chemistry applications.

**MacKnight et al. (2025)** conducted the most comprehensive head-to-head comparison to date, benchmarking LLM-guided optimization (LLM-GO) against BO with Gaussian process regression across six fully enumerated categorical reaction datasets (Suzuki–Miyaura, Buchwald–Hartwig, reductive amination, N-alkylation, deprotection, and a multi-objective Chan–Lam task). They found that frontier LLMs (notably Claude-3.5-sonnet) "consistently match or exceed BO performance across five single-objective datasets," with the advantage growing as task complexity and solution scarcity increase (macknight2025pretrainedknowledgeelevates pages 1-3, macknight2025pretrainedknowledgeelevates pages 15-17). An information-theoretic analysis showed that LLMs maintain higher exploration entropy than BO while achieving better outcomes. However, BO retained superiority for multi-objective problems and when molecular descriptors could be deployed. An important caveat is the risk of data contamination—benchmark datasets may appear in LLM training corpora—and the study was limited to discrete categorical spaces (macknight2025pretrainedknowledgeelevates pages 17-19).

**Wang et al. (2026)** introduced a training-free LLM-based active learning framework (LLM-AL) for materials science, using prompt engineering in place of surrogate model training. LLM-AL often converged faster than classical BO baselines (GPR, random forests, Bayesian neural networks with UCB), reducing experiments to reach top candidates by over 70% in some cases (wang2026trainingfreeactivelearning pages 5-8, wang2026trainingfreeactivelearning pages 1-3). Prompt design proved to be a critical design variable: concise parameter-format prompts worked well for high-dimensional compositional spaces, while narrative report-format prompts helped when procedural descriptors contained key information (wang2026trainingfreeactivelearning pages 3-5).

**Gupta et al. (2025)** offered a more skeptical assessment in "LLMs for Bayesian Optimization in Scientific Domains: Are We There Yet?", finding that instruction-tuned LLMs are largely insensitive to experimental feedback—performance is driven by prior knowledge rather than posterior updating. Classical BO with proper embeddings consistently outperformed LLM-only agents. Their proposed hybrid heuristic (LLM-guided Nearest Neighbour, LLMNN) outperformed the purely LLM-driven BioDiscoveryAgent, suggesting that LLMs are most useful for seeding exploration rather than running the full optimization loop (gupta2509llmsforbayesian pages 7-9, gupta2509llmsforbayesian pages 1-2, gupta2509llmsforbayesian pages 2-4).

## 2. Hybrid LLM/BO Frameworks

The hybrid paradigm—in which LLM-generated hypotheses, domain knowledge, or priors warm-start or steer a BO campaign—has emerged as the dominant approach for combining these technologies.

**BORA (Cissé et al., 2025)** is the Cooper group's Language-Based Bayesian Optimization Research Assistant, a fully automated hybrid framework that maintains a shared GP surrogate updated from both BO-suggested and LLM-suggested experiments. An adaptive trust mechanism scores the quality of LLM interventions, and a plateau-detection heuristic triggers LLM involvement when the search stagnates. The LLM warm-starts optimization by generating initial hypotheses, provides real-time commentary, and suggests new regions for exploration (cisse2025languagebasedbayesianoptimization pages 1-2, cisse2025languagebasedbayesianoptimization pages 4-6, cisse2025languagebasedbayesianoptimization pages 3-4). On a 10-dimensional hydrogen production task, BORA achieved up to 67% improvement over LLM-only optimization and 31% improvement on the 15-dimensional Ackley benchmark (cisse2025languagebasedbayesianoptimization pages 9-12). BORA outperformed HypBO (p = 0.02) and ColaBO on 5 of 6 tasks (cisse2025languagebasedbayesianoptimization pages 7-8).

**The follow-up study** by Cissé, Cooper, Zhu, Evangelopoulos, and Cooper, "Can we automate scientific reasoning in closed-loop experiments using large language models?", published in *Digital Discovery*, 2026 (DOI: 10.1039/D5DD00520E), extends the BORA framework to investigate whether reasoning models can autonomously conduct scientific reasoning within closed-loop experimental campaigns. Although the full text was not obtainable during this review, the paper was identified in search results as a ChemRxiv preprint (DOI: 10.26434/chemrxiv.10001632/v2).

**LLAMBO (Liu et al., 2024)** is a modular framework that integrates LLMs into BO for warmstarting, surrogate modeling, and candidate sampling via in-context learning. Zero-shot warmstarting with LLM-proposed initial points consistently outperformed random initialization, and the LLM surrogate showed better predictive accuracy than GPs when observations were very sparse, though GPs produced superior uncertainty estimates (liu2024largelanguagemodels pages 2-4, liu2024largelanguagemodels pages 1-2, liu2024largelanguagemodels pages 4-6, liu2024largelanguagemodels pages 6-7). A reproducibility study by Rychert et al. (2025) confirmed LLAMBO's benefits but found that its advantages stem primarily from semantic priors across tasks rather than superior single-task regression (rychert2511reproducibilitystudyof pages 6-7, rychert2511reproducibilitystudyof pages 3-4).

**LLINBO (Chang et al., 2025)** provides the first theoretically grounded hybrid framework, proposing three mechanisms—LLINBO-Transient, LLINBO-Justify, and LLINBO-Constrained—each with formal cumulative regret bounds. The framework uses LLMs for early contextual exploration while relying on GP-based UCB for principled exploitation. A proof-of-concept on 3D printing optimization demonstrated near-zero stringing defects (chang2025llinbotrustworthyllmintheloop pages 7-9, chang2025llinbotrustworthyllmintheloop pages 1-3, chang2025llinbotrustworthyllmintheloop pages 3-5).

**ChemBOMAS (Han et al., 2025)** deploys a multi-agent LLM system that decomposes the search space hierarchically using chemical knowledge (coarse stage) and generates LLM-based pseudo-data to warm-start BO within promising subspaces (fine stage). An LLaMa 3.1-based regression model, pre-trained on the Pistachio patent database and fine-tuned with LoRA, provides pseudo-labels that reduce cold-start problems. Wet-lab validation on an industrial reaction found a 96% yield optimum at iteration two, compared with 15% achieved by domain experts (han2025chembomasacceleratedbo pages 5-6, han2025chembomasacceleratedbo pages 1-2, han2025chembomasacceleratedbo pages 8-9).

**Reasoning BO (Yang et al., 2025)** integrates reasoning LLMs, multi-agent verification, and a dual-channel knowledge base (knowledge graph + vector database) into the BO loop. The BO component proposes candidates while the LLM ranks and contextualizes them, avoiding candidate hallucination. On a Direct Arylation task, the framework achieved 60.7% yield versus 25.2% for vanilla BO (yang2025reasoningboenhancing pages 1-3, yang2025reasoningboenhancing pages 5-7).

**Patel et al. (2025)** developed a novel approach of distilling LLM prior knowledge into a quantitative utility function via survey-style pairwise prompts and preference learning (PairwiseGP). The resulting utility function g(x) showed modest correlation with experimental yields even in a zero-shot setting, and when integrated into BO as a constraint on expected improvement, it improved early optimization in 4 of 6 Buchwald–Hartwig and direct arylation datasets (patel2026distillingandexploiting pages 7-12, patel2026distillingandexploiting pages 2-4, patel2026distillingandexploiting pages 1-2).

## 3. LLMs for BO Problem Setup: Design-Space Specification, Feature Selection, and Constraint Elicitation

An underexplored but increasingly important role for LLMs is assisting with the setup of BO campaigns: defining the search space, selecting appropriate descriptors, and eliciting domain constraints.

**Xie (2024)** developed Domain Knowledge Injection Bayesian Optimization (DKIBO) in a thesis at the University of Liverpool. DKIBO uses GPT-4 to analyze historical data and propose statistical relationships between features as auxiliary predictive models, which are incorporated as additive terms in the BO acquisition function to improve GP fitting without inducing problematic length scales. The system also fine-tuned GPT-3 on SMILES strings for molecular property classification, demonstrating how LLMs can serve as both feature proposers and model augmenters in the problem-setup phase (xie2024chemicalspaceexploration pages 1-6, xie2024chemicalspaceexploration pages 26-30, xie2024chemicalspaceexploration pages 6-11).

**ChemBOMAS (Han et al., 2025)** addresses design-space specification through its coarse-grained stage, in which LLM agents analyze literature and physicochemical data to rank reaction parameters by importance, perform clustering, and build a hierarchical optimization tree that decomposes the original search space into manageable subspaces. This is a concrete example of LLM-driven search-space reduction for BO (han2025chembomasacceleratedbo pages 2-5).

**The BO Hackathon for Chemistry and Materials (Baird et al., 2025)** featured several LLM-related projects addressing problem setup. SimpleGPT-BO integrates ChatGPT to make BO accessible without installation, supporting multi-objective optimization. BOPE-GPT uses GPT-4 for pairwise preference elicitation within preferential BO. RAMBO (Retrieval-Augmented BO) begins by describing the design space of a reaction and queries existing data to present starting conditions with explanations. BlendDS provides intuitive specification of design spaces for blends of components. Together, these hackathon projects demonstrate how LLMs can lower the barrier for non-experts to set up and run BO campaigns (baird2025bayesianoptimizationhackathon pages 16-17, baird2025bayesianoptimizationhackathon pages 7-9, baird2025bayesianoptimizationhackathon pages 11-12, baird2025bayesianoptimizationhackathon pages 3-6).

## 4. LLM-Based and Foundation-Model Surrogates Inside BO Loops

The use of LLMs as surrogate models within BO—either as fixed feature extractors, in-context regressors, or jointly optimized deep kernels—has been perhaps the most technically active subfield, accompanied by critical assessments of whether these approaches genuinely improve on classical baselines.

**Kristiadi et al. (2024)** provide the seminal "sober look" at this question. By treating LLMs as fixed feature extractors for principled BO surrogates and by using parameter-efficient fine-tuning with Bayesian neural networks for uncertainty estimation, they found that LLMs can be useful for molecular BO—but only if they are domain-specific (chemistry-pretrained) or fine-tuned. General-purpose LLM features (T5, GPT-2, Llama-2) generally underperformed even simple 1024-bit fingerprints, while chemistry-specific T5-Chem was the best overall. The study warns that "LLMs often produce convincing but incorrect chemical answers," motivating principled Bayesian treatment rather than naive in-context approaches (kristiadi2024asoberlook pages 8-9, kristiadi2024asoberlook pages 5-7, kristiadi2024asoberlook pages 1-2).

**GOLLuM (Ranković & Schwaller, 2025)** responds to these concerns by reframing LLM fine-tuning as GP marginal likelihood optimization through deep kernel learning. By jointly optimizing LLM embeddings and GP hyperparameters, GOLLuM creates an implicit contrastive learning effect that organizes the latent space by function value. Across 19 chemistry benchmarks, GOLLuM achieved a 23% increase in covering high-output regions and nearly doubled the discovery rate of top-performing Buchwald–Hartwig reactions (from 24% to 43% coverage of top 5% in 50 iterations). Importantly, the method works with general-purpose LLMs without domain-specific pretraining (rankovic2504gollumgaussianprocess pages 9-10, rankovic2504gollumgaussianprocess pages 1-2, rankovic2504gollumgaussianprocess pages 6-8, rankovic2504gollumgaussianprocess pages 4-6).

**Ramos et al. (2026)** demonstrated BO with in-context learning (BO-ICL) for catalysis, using frozen LLMs as surrogate models by converting experimental procedures into natural-language prompts. On an oxidative coupling of methane benchmark, GPT-4 variants reached the 99th percentile of candidates with only 10 additional samples, performing on par with GP regression. In real reverse water-gas shift experiments, BO-ICL found near-optimal catalysts within six iterations. However, the authors note that LLM uncertainty estimates are often poorly calibrated (especially for RLHF models), and pretrained biases can push suggestions toward well-studied materials (ramos2026bayesianoptimizationof pages 1-3, ramos2026bayesianoptimizationof pages 5-7, ramos2026bayesianoptimizationof pages 9-10, ramos2026bayesianoptimizationof pages 10-12).

**Embed-then-Regress (Nguyen et al., 2024)** proposes embedding arbitrary string inputs with a pretrained T5-XL encoder and performing regression with a small in-context Transformer trained on synthetic BBOB trajectories. The approach achieves performance comparable to industry GP-Bandit baselines across synthetic, combinatorial, and hyperparameter optimization tasks, demonstrating that string-based in-context regression can be a practical general-purpose surrogate (nguyen2024predictingfromstrings pages 7-9, nguyen2024predictingfromstrings pages 9-11, nguyen2024predictingfromstrings pages 1-3, nguyen2024predictingfromstrings pages 5-7).

**LICO (Nguyen & Grover, 2025)** extends base LLMs with separate embedding and prediction layers trained on diverse molecular functions, achieving state-of-the-art on the PMO benchmark for molecular optimization. The model generalizes to unseen properties via in-context prompting.

**Akke/Gómez-Bombarelli et al. (2025/2026)** provide a systematic analysis of success and failure modes across molecular and protein optimization tasks. Off-the-shelf LLMs struggle with SMILES-heavy molecular tasks due to poor data comprehension and limited in-context data processing capacity, but reasoning LLMs excel at hypothesis generation for protein binding motif optimization. A critical finding is that LLMs tend to overfixate on irrelevant context—withholding information can paradoxically improve performance. They recommend agentic workflows combining LLM reasoning with domain tools and statistical acquisition functions, and show that fine-tuning on synthetic BO trajectories can instill Bayesian-like behavior (gomezbombarelli2026bayesianoptimizationfor pages 1-3, akke2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 1-2, akke2025bayesianoptimizationfor pages 2-3).

## 5. Agentic and Closed-Loop Laboratory Systems Combining LLM Planning with BO-Driven Experiment Selection

The vision of fully autonomous "Self-Driving Laboratory 2.0" systems that integrate LLM-driven planning, knowledge extraction, and decision-making with BO-guided experiment selection is rapidly materializing.

**Reasoning BO (Yang et al., 2025)**, discussed in Section 2, is also relevant here as it implements a multi-agent pipeline with generation, verification, formatting, and note-taking agents that operate within a closed BO loop, continuously enriching a knowledge graph and vector database with verified experimental insights (yang2025reasoningboenhancing pages 1-3, yang2025reasoningboenhancing pages 5-7).

**Lee et al. (2026)** outline six defining characteristics of SDL 2.0: interoperable, collaborative, generalizable, orchestrated, safe, and creative. Their review surveys the integration of robotics, BO, AI-driven computer vision, and LLMs to enable fully autonomous closed-loop experimentation, including chemically informed BO approaches (ChIDDO) that embed domain constraints into surrogates (lee2026towardselfdrivinglaboratory pages 7-8).

**Hao et al. (2025)** provide a comprehensive review of autonomous materials synthesis laboratories, analyzing robotic hardware, integrated analytical instrumentation, closed-loop BO/AL planners, AI-driven decision-making, and multi-agent coordination systems. They note that LLMs have recently enhanced these systems by improving knowledge extraction, experimental planning, and multi-agent coordination, while BO remains the primary optimization engine (hao2025autonomousmaterialssynthesis pages 68-71, hao2025autonomousmaterialssynthesis pages 14-16, hao2025autonomousmaterialssynthesis pages 1-4).

**Su et al. (2024)** review how LLMs are reshaping catalyst design through four key components: high-throughput information extraction, automated robotic experimentation, real-time feedback for iterative BO-driven optimization, and interpretable machine learning. They highlight LLM agents (Coscientist, ChemCrow) as planning and control modules that interface with robotic synthesis platforms and BO decision engines (chen2025artificialintelligencedrivenautonomous pages 1-3).

**ChemBOMAS (Han et al., 2025)** also represents a concrete agentic system, with its multi-agent architecture combining literature-analysis agents, search-space decomposition agents, and BO agents that collaborate dynamically for wet-lab-validated reaction optimization (han2025chembomasacceleratedbo pages 1-2, han2025chembomasacceleratedbo pages 8-9).
