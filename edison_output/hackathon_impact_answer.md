# Scientific Hackathons and Community Coding Events as a Model for Research Training and Open-Source Output in Computational Science, Chemistry, Materials, and Machine Learning: A Literature Review

## 1. Introduction

Scientific hackathons—time-bounded, intensive collaborative events in which participants form teams to develop software prototypes, analyses, or solutions to domain-specific challenges—have emerged as a distinctive model for research training, community building, and open-source software production across the computational sciences. This review synthesizes evidence from reported hackathons in chemistry, materials science, bioinformatics, toxicology, ecology, and machine learning, with particular attention to measurable outcomes, follow-up impact, the role of participant expertise, best practices for organization, and the growing role of large language model (LLM) hackathons.

## 2. Measurable Outcomes and Lessons Learned

### 2.1 Tangible Software and Scientific Products

Hackathons consistently produce working software prototypes, pipelines, and datasets, often within 24–48 hours. The first materials science hackathon, MatHack, held at the 2014 MRS Fall Meeting, demonstrated that 14 materials scientists working for 24 hours could produce functional tools including a three-dimensional crystal viewer, DFT approaches for quasicrystals, and machine-learning-based materials design tools (mulholland2015hackathonaimsto pages 1-2). Similarly, an NIH-hosted bioinformatics hackathon produced four complete genomic analysis pipelines (DNA-seq, epigenomics, metagenomics, and RNA-seq), all published on GitHub under Creative Commons Attribution licensing (busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 8-12, busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 1-4, busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 29-32).

The Hacktive Matter series of hackathons in soft matter/active matter research yielded BARCODE (Biomaterial Activity Readouts to Categorize, Optimize, Design and Engineer), an integrated software package, along with peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations that accelerated the research program substantially (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 8-10). The organizers identified an optimal event duration of approximately 2.5 days to maintain productivity while avoiding diminishing returns (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 12-14).

In the LLM hackathon domain, the first LLM Hackathon for Applications in Materials Science and Chemistry produced 14 working prototypes in under two days across four categories: predictive modeling, automation and novel interfaces, knowledge extraction, and education (jablonka202314examplesof pages 4-7, jablonka202314examplesof pages 1-4). The authors concluded that similar projects would typically require many months without the rapid prototyping enabled by LLMs (jablonka202314examplesof pages 24-28). The second iteration (2024) scaled dramatically to 556 registered participants and 34 team submissions spanning seven application areas, while also establishing a persistent Slack community of 483 researchers (zimmermann2024reflectionsfromthe pages 7-10, zimmermann2024reflectionsfromthe pages 3-5).

The community-engaged test set approach introduced by Wu et al. (2026) represents an innovative variant: a Kaggle competition for photostability prediction in light-harvesting molecules received 729 submissions from 522 entrants within one month, despite only $150 in total prizes. The top-performing community model achieved a mean squared log error of 1.026, substantially improving upon the in-house model's score of 3.051, and participants discovered new chemical insights including a negative correlation between pyridine rings and photostability (wu2026democratizingmachinelearning pages 4-5, wu2026democratizingmachinelearning pages 1-2).

### 2.2 Lessons Learned from Organizing

The ONTOX Hackathon report provides one of the most detailed organizational retrospectives. Key lessons included the need for stronger recruitment of regulatory experts and policymakers, the importance of structured sprint processes with professional coaching, and careful calibration of jury involvement—jury discussions were found to sometimes overly influence participant direction. The organizers recommended reducing jury visits to one per group with a maximum of two jury members, allocating keynote speakers to the first day, and implementing cloud-based rapporteur reporting systems (diemar2025reportofthe pages 8-10, diemar2025reportofthe pages 7-8). The event required over one year of preparation (diemar2025reportofthe pages 8-10).

The EcoHack (2025) organizers reported that teams did not form before the event as expected, suggesting that virtual meet-and-greet sessions one to two weeks prior could improve this process. They also found that access to domain experts, while valued, was underutilized, and recommended structured formats such as expert Q&A sessions and mentoring hours (dsouza2025reflectionsfromthe pages 7-8). A key lesson from the bioinformatics hackathon was the significant time consumed by data quality issues and corrupted files from public repositories, suggesting that pre-hackathon data validation is essential (busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 17-21, busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 21-25).

## 3. Follow-Up Impact: Post-Event Adoption and Community Persistence

Longitudinal follow-up data on whether hackathon participants subsequently adopt methods in their own research remains limited in the published literature, representing a recognized gap. The most quantitative evidence comes from the Hacktive Matter series, where post-hackathon surveys indicated that nearly 50% of participants anticipated immediately applying learned skills upon returning to their laboratories, and over 80% anticipated using them within one year (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 10-12). Returning participants in subsequent hackathon iterations became "ambassadors" who transferred knowledge within the broader research collaboration and maintained momentum between events (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 12-14).

The 2024 LLM Hackathon for Materials Science and Chemistry demonstrated community persistence through the growth of a Slack community of 483 researchers that continued to operate after the event (zimmermann2024reflectionsfromthe pages 7-10). The MatHack organizers noted that "the collaborations and relationships that formed at MatHack...will certainly continue throughout the participants' scientific careers" and that some projects were likely to continue development (mulholland2015hackathonaimsto pages 2-3). The bioinformatics hackathon similarly reported continued post-event collaboration among participants (busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 25-29).

At the community-software scale, the RosettaCommons experience over two decades demonstrates that hackathon-like intensive coding workshops (eXtreme Rosetta Workshops, or XRWs) played an important role in maintaining and advancing a codebase of over 3.1 million lines of code developed by more than 800 scientists from over 60 institutions (leman2020bettertogetherelements pages 1-2, leman2020bettertogetherelements pages 4-6). These workshops were credited with significantly enhancing code maintainability, extensibility, and community spirit (leman2020bettertogetherelements pages 11-13).

## 4. Prior Participant Expertise and Its Relationship to Outcomes

### 4.1 Skill Diversity as a Strength

Multiple studies converge on the finding that heterogeneous teams—mixing participants with different levels and types of expertise—produce stronger outcomes than homogeneous groups. The Hacktive Matter hackathons deliberately assembled mixed-skill teams and positioned skill diversity as a strength, noting that novice participants brought fresh perspectives and enthusiasm while experienced participants provided technical leadership. Over multiple years, some participants progressed from beginners to specialists who could lead prototyping activities (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 12-14, valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 10-12, valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 8-10). The event design accommodated different skill levels by offering problems with multiple solution paths of varying technical difficulty (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 10-12).

In the LLM hackathon context, the combination of diverse expertise areas with tight deadlines and ease of LLM prototyping was described as crucial—generating both novel research questions and pragmatic solutions that neither domain experts nor computational specialists alone would have produced (jablonka202314examplesof pages 4-7). The bioinformatics hackathon similarly found that diverse team backgrounds promoted innovation through consensus-building (busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 25-29), and the MatHack report emphasized that participants ranged from "experienced computational materials scientists with tremendous coding backgrounds" to "experimentalists with practical problems to solve" (mulholland2015hackathonaimsto pages 1-2).

### 4.2 Design Thinking and Informal Expertise

A study of 37 interviews with hackathon winners and organizers from 16 countries found that winning teams, despite lacking formal Design Thinking training, intuitively applied design methods through divergent-convergent thinking phases. Multidisciplinary team composition positively affected outcomes, with diverse teams (IT, design, business) exploring user research and business models more thoroughly than tech-focused groups (gama2023thedevelopers’design pages 1-3, gama2023thedevelopers’design pages 13-15). Critically, the study found that a design thinking *mindset*—reflecting on problems before jumping to solutions—was more important than formal methodological training (gama2023thedevelopers’design pages 13-15). Winners learned design methods from peers during events through learn-by-doing approaches (gama2023thedevelopers’design pages 3-5).

## 5. Best Practices for Judging, Mentoring, Team Formation, and Post-Event Archiving

### 5.1 Team Formation

Effective team formation strategies vary by hackathon type. MatHack used a pitch-and-network approach: participants gave 30-second idea pitches, followed by 30 minutes of networking before self-selecting into teams around ideas (mulholland2015hackathonaimsto pages 1-2). The ONTOX Hackathon prioritized diversity and participant preferences, intentionally embedding early-career researchers from the host project in each group to ensure domain knowledge transfer, with groups of up to seven individuals across eight breakout teams (diemar2025reportofthe pages 5-7). The Hacktive Matter hackathons assigned groups of 3–4 with deliberately mixed coding expertise levels so all members could contribute meaningfully across roles including data curation, coding, testing, and documentation (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 5-8). The bioinformatics hackathon assigned structured team roles covering systems administration, metadata management, data acquisition, normalization, downstream analysis, and documentation, selecting from 131 qualified applicants (busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 8-12).

### 5.2 Mentoring

Mentoring structures range from embedded faculty leads to professional coaches. The Hacktive Matter series assigned 2–3 faculty leads per group who provided guidance, monitored progress, and facilitated problem-solving, with structured report-outs after sessions serving as feedback mechanisms (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 5-8). The ONTOX Hackathon employed two professional coaches for overall guidance, teambuilding activities, and pacing, supplemented by ten subject-matter experts available for non-intrusive consultation (diemar2025reportofthe pages 4-5, diemar2025reportofthe pages 5-7). For online/hybrid events, the Mendes et al. (2022) study of four online hackathons found that mentor accessibility was problematic in virtual settings, recommending more proactive mentoring approaches (mendes2022sociotechnicalconstraintsand pages 25-26). The EcoHack organizers recommended structured expert access through Q&A sessions and dedicated mentoring hours (dsouza2025reflectionsfromthe pages 7-8).

The RosettaCommons model provides a long-term perspective: their Code School courses feature a high teacher-to-student ratio (~1:5), and senior developers with 6+ years of experience play crucial roles in advancing the codebase and mentoring newcomers (leman2020bettertogetherelements pages 11-13). The community also established formal structures including a Code of Conduct and diversity statement (leman2020bettertogetherelements pages 11-13, leman2020bettertogetherelements pages 13-14).

### 5.3 Judging

Judging approaches in scientific hackathons typically involve domain-expert panels evaluating pitch presentations. MatHack assembled a "diverse group of judges from across the materials community" who selected the top three teams for cash prizes after morning presentations (mulholland2015hackathonaimsto pages 1-2). The ONTOX Hackathon used a four-person jury of academic and industry experts who monitored progress during the event and evaluated final pitches, selecting winners for both best proposals and best pitches (diemar2025reportofthe pages 7-8, diemar2025reportofthe pages 4-5). A key lesson was that jury discussions could overly influence participant direction, and the organizers recommended limiting jury visits to minimize this effect (diemar2025reportofthe pages 8-10).

The design thinking study found that successful hackathon participants strategically framed jury members as central users and aligned project desirability with judge expectations, using techniques such as bold problem statements and memorable pitch takeaways (gama2023thedevelopers’design pages 29-31). Understanding evaluation criteria was identified as a key recommendation for successful participation (gama2023thedevelopers’design pages 27-29).

### 5.4 Post-Event Archiving

Archiving practices vary substantially. The bioinformatics hackathon set clear requirements: all code was published on GitHub with separate repositories for each team, and pipelines were released under Creative Commons Attribution (CC-BY) licensing (busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 8-12, busby2015buildinggenomicanalysis pages 1-4). The Hacktive Matter series utilized cloud-based tools including Slack, GitHub, and Google Drive for real-time documentation, capturing questions, successes, and critical information that could be retained and revisited across multiple years of hackathons (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 5-8). The 2024 LLM Hackathon published team summaries with links to code in a consolidated format (zimmermann2024reflectionsfromthe pages 10-12). The ONTOX Hackathon kept formal rapporteur records and planned post-event follow-up via LinkedIn groups and email updates (diemar2025reportofthe pages 8-10). The RosettaCommons model demonstrates long-term archival through version-controlled repositories, integrated testing frameworks, and collaborative wikis that were expanded during workshop-style events (leman2020bettertogetherelements pages 15-17).

## 6. Chemistry/Materials Science Hackathon Reports

The materials science hackathon tradition began with MatHack at the 2014 MRS Fall Meeting, where 14 participants produced tools including Phone-on Flow (a 3D crystal viewer using Google Cardboard), MatHack QuasiCrystal (DFT approaches for quasicrystals), and Directed Materials Design (machine-learning models for perovskite water-splitting materials) (mulholland2015hackathonaimsto pages 1-2, mulholland2015hackathonaimsto pages 2-3). The MatDat18 hackathon (Ferguson et al., 2019; DOI: 10.1039/C9ME90018G) subsequently brought together materials and data science communities, though this paper was not directly accessible for this review.

The Hacktive Matter hackathon series (Valentine and Robertson-Anderson, 2025) developed BARCODE, a high-throughput biomaterials analysis software package, through a multi-year iterative approach that combined workshops with post-event development sprints (valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 8-10, valentine2025hacktivematterdatadriven pages 1-3). The ONTOX Hackathon (Diemar et al., 2025) focused on AI applications in toxicology and chemical risk assessment, generating proposals such as the "NAMs Wizard" for facilitating the transition from animal testing to human-relevant methods (diemar2025reportofthe pages 8-10, diemar2025reportofthe pages 1-4). Van Herck et al. (2025) built on the LLM hackathon work to systematically assess fine-tuned LLMs across diverse chemistry and materials science tasks, finding that fine-tuning even with small datasets leads to predictive models that often outperform traditional ML approaches for classification problems.

## 7. Large Language Model Hackathon Reports

The LLM hackathon format for scientific applications was pioneered by Jablonka et al. (2023), whose first LLM Hackathon for Applications in Materials Science and Chemistry brought together researchers from 22 institutions across 8 countries. Participants leveraged GPT-4 and related models to develop prototypes for molecular property prediction, novel tool interfaces, knowledge extraction from unstructured data, and educational applications. The time-constrained environment with low failure costs generated more energy and motivation than typical settings, and the projects demonstrated that LLMs did not rely on chemistry-specific knowledge but instead leveraged general reasoning abilities supplemented with domain information through context or fine-tuning (jablonka202314examplesof pages 4-7, jablonka202314examplesof pages 24-28).

The second iteration (Zimmermann et al., 2024) expanded dramatically with 556 registered participants and 34 team submissions organized across a hybrid format with physical hubs in six cities and a global online hub. Projects spanned molecular and material property prediction, molecular design, automation, scientific communication and education, research data management, hypothesis generation, and knowledge extraction. Notable improvements in LLM capabilities between the two hackathons were observed, directly benefiting project outcomes (zimmermann2024reflectionsfromthe pages 7-10, zimmermann2024reflectionsfromthe pages 3-5, zimmermann2024reflectionsfromthe pages 10-12).

The EcoHack (D'Souza et al., 2025) adapted the LLM hackathon model for ecological research, generating 11 team submissions across six application areas including AI-enhanced decision support, knowledge extraction, ecological modeling, and biodiversity monitoring (dsouza2025reflectionsfromthe pages 7-8). This event demonstrated the transferability of the LLM hackathon format across scientific disciplines.

## 8. Gaps and Future Directions

Several gaps emerge from this review. First, longitudinal follow-up data on post-hackathon method adoption remains sparse; most studies rely on self-reported intent rather than measured adoption. Second, while several studies note that diverse teams produce better outcomes, rigorous quantitative analysis of the relationship between specific expertise profiles and project quality is largely absent. Third, standardized metrics for hackathon success beyond counts of projects, participants, and publications have not been established across the field. Fourth, the literature on post-event archiving and code sustainability suggests that many hackathon projects do not continue beyond the event (referenced by Nolte et al., 2020; DOI: 10.1145/3415216, though this paper was not directly accessible). Finally, the multidisciplinary literature review by Chau and Gerber (2023; DOI: 10.1145/3544548.3581234) and the NESCent lessons reported by Stoltzfus et al. (2017; DOI: 10.12688/f1000research.11429.1) represent important broader frameworks that were not directly accessible for this review but are consistently cited across the hackathon literature.
